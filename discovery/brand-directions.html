Brand discovery · working brief

MoiVoyage: the questions, and three ways it could go

A bespoke travel assistant that takes the logistics off your plate so the trip is about the adventure. Two prototypes already exist. This brief turns them into the questions a brand needs answered, states a working assumption for each, and then proposes three distinct directions to react to.

1 October 2026 · for Konstantin · read time about 15 minutes

What the prototypes already say

Before the questions, here is what I took from the two repos. These are the facts the assumptions below lean on.

  • Every trip gets its own world. Daren's Last Ride is a 1973 rock tour: laminates, tickets, setlists, a dark tolex ground. The Green Road is a storybook: letters, envelopes, pastel drawings, a photograph behind the screen. Same engine, two completely different skins.
  • The assistant is a character, and the character lives in the prompt. Lester Bangs, the cantankerous critic. The Seanchaí, who writes in pencil until one of you inks it in. Neither calls itself an AI. Both are grounded: they never invent a time, a venue, or a person, and they say so in character when the data is silent.
  • Committed versus maybe is the information architecture. Tickets versus setlist scribbles. Ink versus pencil. The visual gap between the two is the product's clearest idea, and it carries straight into a brand promise: you always know what is settled and what is still open.
  • Decoration never carries information. Both design docs have this rule and both measure contrast. The brand has to be loud somewhere without ever making a fact harder to read.
  • The product plan already separates four layers: function, capability profile, skin, and trip configuration. A theme is a data package, generated by a skill and policed by tests. That means "a bespoke app per trip" is not a slogan, it is a thing you can actually ship, and it is the strongest claim available to this brand.

The questions

Nineteen questions in five groups. Six are tagged decide first because the three directions fork on them. Each one carries the assumption I used to draft the directions, so you can answer by number, or just correct the assumptions you disagree with.

A. Who it is for

1Who is the first traveller MoiVoyage is built for?

A brand for the two of you, for friends you organise trips for, or for paying strangers reads differently in every line of copy. Lester can be rude to his friends. A stranger's assistant cannot.

AssumptionFriends and friends-of-friends first, with paying strangers in view. Warmth allowed, insults not.

2What shape of trip is the home case?

A three-week, two-person itinerary with lodgings and trains is a different product from a four-day group weekend with a crew and a kitty. Both prototypes exist; which one is the centre?

AssumptionLeisure trips of three days to three weeks, two to twelve people, where someone cares enough to plan.

3How is it paid for, if at all?

Per-trip, subscription, or free changes how premium the brand should feel and whether "bespoke" is a price or a personality. The plan already talks about putting trips on sale.

AssumptionPaid per voyage. One trip, one price, one world. That makes "bespoke" literal.

B. What it is

4Is MoiVoyage one brand, or a house that makes a world per trip?

Penguin's logo sits on a thousand different covers. Apple's does not. Your prototypes are the Penguin model: the trip is the cover, and the brand is the colophon in the corner. Choosing this decides how visible, and how neutral, the house look has to be.

AssumptionA house with a per-trip skin. The brand is recognised by its mark and its manners, not by a colour.

5Is the assistant the product, or a feature of it?

If the assistant is the product, it needs its own name and face, and the app is its home. If it is a feature, the app is the product and the assistant is one of its tools, like the map.

AssumptionThe app is the product. The assistant is its most important feature and has a name, which may change per trip (the Seanchaí, Lester).

6How much does it do without being asked?

A butler acts and reports. A companion notices and suggests. A desk waits for the bell. Autonomy is the single biggest lever on trust, and later on commerce: an assistant that rebooks your train is a very different promise from one that tells you the platform changed.

AssumptionIt notices and suggests, and it acts only on things it was told are its to handle. Nothing is booked, moved, or paid for without a tap.

7What will it never do?

Both prototypes refuse to guess. A brand that says "I don't know, and I'm not guessing" is rarer than one that says "consider it handled." Which refusals are part of the character?

AssumptionNever invents a fact. Never pretends to have done something. Never nags. Never sells you something it wasn't asked for.

C. The voice

8Does it say "I"?

Lester and the Seanchaí both speak in the first person as characters. A house brand usually speaks as "we" or not at all. A single companion says "I" every time. This is the voice question underneath all the others.

AssumptionThe house says "we" sparingly. Inside a trip, the named assistant says "I".

9Where is the dial between warm and witty?

Lester is funny at the user's expense. The Seanchaí is gentle. Can MoiVoyage tease, or only delight?

AssumptionDry rather than jokey. It can be amused; it is never sarcastic about the traveller.

10Does it have taste?

The Seanchaí leans toward natural wine bars and small neighbourhoods. An assistant with opinions is a brand with a point of view, and later a commerce engine with a bias. Is the taste yours, the traveller's, or none?

AssumptionIt has taste it learned from the traveller, and it says where a suggestion came from.

11How does it speak when something goes wrong?

A delayed flight is the moment the brand is actually tested. Calm and specific, or sympathetic and chatty?

AssumptionCalm first, specific second, sympathy in one clause at most. The fix is the comfort.

12How French is it?

The name is French. Does any French live in the interface (bon voyage, en route, à table), or is the name the only trace? And the grammar point: in French it would be "mon voyage". "Moi, voyage" reads as a playful "me, travel". Embrace the wink or stay away from it?

AssumptionThe name is the only French. Treat "Moi" as English-speakers use it, a little theatrical, a little self-centred, in a good way: this trip is about you.

D. The look

13Physical-world metaphor, or digital-native?

Both prototypes are built from paper: laminates, letters, envelopes, washi tape. It is your strongest visual habit and it is distinctive. A brand built on it looks like nothing else in the category. It also ages a product toward nostalgia.

AssumptionPhysical at the trip level, where each world can pick its own materials. The house itself stays quieter.

14Illustration, photography, or neither?

The Green Road draws every city. That is expensive per trip, unless the illustration script scales, which it seems to. Is drawing part of the promise?

AssumptionDrawing is a signature of the house. Every voyage gets at least one drawn thing.

15How loud?

Last Ride is locked at full maximalism. The Green Road is quiet. The brand needs a default volume, even if each trip can turn it up or down.

AssumptionQuiet house, loud when the trip asks for it.

16What must it never look like?

A list of anti-references is often more useful than references. Chat bubbles with a sparkle icon? Airline apps? Travel-booking sites with urgency banners?

AssumptionNever a chatbot. Never a booking site. Never a dashboard.

E. The name and what comes next

17How is the name set?

MoiVoyage, Moi Voyage, moi·voyage, MOI VOYAGE. One word says software. Two words say a house. The dot says a wink. Also: has the domain and mark been checked?

AssumptionOne word in prose, and the wordmark is free to split it. Domain and trademark unchecked.

18When it starts finding deals and booking tables, how should money feel?

A concierge is discreet about money. A friend is transparent about it ("they gave me 20% off, you're welcome"). A butler never mentions it. The brand should decide now, before the feature exists, because it shapes what people trust the assistant with.

AssumptionTransparent. It says what it found, what it costs, and who benefits. Trust is the business.

19What does success look like in a year?

"Our friends ask us to make their trip app" is a different brand from "ten thousand strangers paid for one." The first can be strange and personal. The second needs an identity that survives being seen for the first time.

AssumptionDozens of trips for people you know, a handful for people you don't. Personal, not mass.

How to answer: reply with the numbers that matter and a line each. Anything you skip, I keep the assumption. The six tagged questions (1, 4, 5, 6, 8, 13) are the ones that pick a direction.

Three directions

Each direction answers the six forking questions differently. They are deliberately far apart: a house that makes worlds, a single companion with a face, and a service with a desk. Each one shows its wordmark, palette, type, a phone screen for the same moment on the same trip (Wednesday 14 October, the train from York to Edinburgh), and the same five lines of copy so the voices can be compared side by side.

Direction A · the house

The Atelier

MVMoi Voyage

MoiVoyage is a small house that makes one world per trip. The brand is the colophon in the corner: a mark, a set of manners, and a quiet off-white ground that lets each voyage's own skin be the loud thing.

The idea

The analogy is a publisher. Penguin's orange bird sits on every book, and no two covers match. MoiVoyage's mark sits on every trip, and no two trips look alike. The Green Road and Daren's Last Ride are already two covers. The brand is what they have in common: the care, the ink-and-pencil rule, a named narrator, a drawn thing, and a promise that the facts are always legible.

Brand architecture

House voice for onboarding, settings, billing and the welcome. Inside a trip, the trip's own narrator speaks. The house never interrupts a trip in its own voice, the way a publisher never writes in the margins.

The signature

A passport stamp. Each voyage gets one, rotated, in the house red, carrying the trip's name and dates. It appears once on the cover of the trip and once at the end, as the souvenir. It is the only place the house colour appears inside a trip.

Where commerce fits

The house never sells. The narrator finds. A deal is reported the way a time is: as a fact with a source, in pencil until you ink it. "Thistle Street will do 15% off the tasting menu for a Tuesday booking" is a pencil line, not a banner.

Why this, and the risk

  • Choose it if the answer to question 4 is "a house". It is the only direction that keeps both prototypes as they are. It is the most defensible claim: nobody else can honestly say every trip gets its own app.
  • The risk is invisibility. A brand that stays out of the way can be forgotten. The stamp and the mark have to work hard, and the house voice has to be good enough in its few lines that people quote it.
  • It depends on the theme-generating skill being real and good. If the house cannot make a world per trip cheaply, the brand promise is a lie.
Direction B · the companion

Moi, the friend who's been everywhere

moivoyage

The assistant is the brand, and the brand has a name: Moi. One character, one voice, one face, in every trip. The app is Moi's home, and the trip is something you and Moi are doing together.

The idea

Everyone has one friend who has been everywhere and never makes you feel bad about it. They know the platform change before the board does, they know the bar with the fire, and they text you about it at the right moment, not every moment. Moi is that friend. "Moi" works on both levels: it is the French "me" in the name, and it is the name of the one who makes the trip about you.

Brand architecture

One brand, one voice. Trips can change colour and motif the way a friend changes clothes, but Moi's handwriting, Moi's face and Moi's way of speaking never change. The per-trip skin becomes a lighter layer than it is today: a palette and a few drawings, not a new narrator.

The signature

Stickers. Every place, every settled plan, every thing Moi handled gets a sticker, slightly crooked, slightly shadowed, the way a suitcase collects them. The wordmark itself is a sticker. Facts stay on the grid in a hyper-legible face; the stickers are never the only place a fact lives.

Where commerce fits

Moi is transparent about money because friends are. "They gave me 15% off because it's a Tuesday" is a line Moi can say with pride. Moi never earns on a recommendation without saying so, and the brand's whole value depends on that staying true.

Why this, and the risk

  • Choose it if the answer to question 5 is "the assistant is the product." It is the warmest, most memorable and most marketable direction. People can draw Moi. People will quote Moi.
  • The risk is that one voice cannot be the Seanchaí and Lester at once. A bachelor party and an anniversary trip want different friends. Moi has to be flexible enough to be dry at a wedding and loud at a ballgame without breaking character.
  • It changes the prototypes. The Green Road's narrator would become Moi in a storybook outfit. Some of the per-trip magic moves from the character to the costume.
Direction C · the service

The Desk

MMoiVoyage

MoiVoyage is a concierge desk you carry. Bottle green, cream, a little brass. It speaks like the best hotel desk you have ever met: it already knows, it has already called, and it would never dream of making a fuss.

The idea

The bespoke promise taken at its word. Not a friend, not a publisher: a service. The desk is where you leave your keys and your worries. It is discreet, which is the opposite of a chatbot, and it is confident, which is what logistics anxiety actually needs. It is the direction most ready for commerce, because concierges have always booked tables and known people.

Brand architecture

One brand, one register. Each trip gets its own stationery (a monogram, a colour of key tag, a drawn crest) the way each hotel in a group has its own letterhead, but the manners are the house's. The assistant is "the Desk", and it does not need a first name, though a trip may give it one.

The signature

The brass key tag. Every trip has one, numbered, with the trip's name engraved. The tag is the app icon, the loading state, and the thing you tap to ring the desk. Settled plans are engraved; open ones are typed on a slip tucked behind the tag.

Voice

The desk speaks in short declaratives, mostly in the second person, and refers to itself as "the desk" or not at all. It reports what it did, then what it did not do. It never uses an exclamation mark. It is warm the way a good hotel is warm: by remembering, not by gushing.

First openWelcome. The desk has read your voyage: twenty-two days, seven places. Four things are settled, nine are held, two need you. Shall we begin with those two?

Where commerce fits

Naturally, and that is the point. A desk that knows people and holds tables is the oldest version of what an agent of commerce does. Money is handled discreetly: the price is stated once, the desk's cut (if any) is stated once, and neither is repeated.

Why this, and the risk

  • Choose it if the answer to question 3 is "paid, and worth it", and the answer to question 6 leans toward "it acts." It is the most grown-up direction and the one that sells itself in a sentence.
  • The risk is the category. "AI concierge" is what every travel start-up says, and bottle-green-and-brass is what every luxury brand looks like. This direction only works if the execution is strange enough to be yours: the key tag, the held slips, the refusal to gush.
  • It is the least fun. The brief asks for fun and adventure. A desk is calm. Adventure would have to come from the trips, not the house.

Where I would start

Direction A, with B's warmth in the house voice. The per-trip world is the thing you have already proven twice and that nobody else can claim, so the brand should be built to protect it. The house stays quiet so the trips can be loud.

The one thing to steal from B is the welcome: the house's few lines should feel like a person made this for you, not like a publisher's imprint page. And the one thing to steal from C is the refusal to gush, which both prototypes already have.

If you answer question 5 with "the assistant is the product," switch to B and let Moi wear each trip as a costume. If you answer question 3 with "expensive," look harder at C.

What happens after you answer

  1. You answer the numbered questions, or correct the assumptions.
  2. I fold the answers into one chosen direction and write the brand book: name treatment, mark, palette with measured contrast, type, voice principles, a copy deck for the twenty most common moments, and the rules a per-trip skin may and may not break.
  3. Then the first house-level artefact: the welcome flow, the stamp or tag or sticker, and the house voice, built as a theme package the existing skill can generate against.
